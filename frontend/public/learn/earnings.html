Reading an earnings report

Intermediate · Equity research

The idea

Every quarter, public companies report their results: revenue, profit, earnings per share (EPS) and, often, a forecast for the next period (guidance). Investors compare these with what was expected, because the share price already reflects those expectations.

How it works

  • Revenue: sales for the quarter. EPS: profit per share. Check both against the same quarter a year earlier, not just the previous quarter, because many businesses are seasonal.
  • Versus expectations: analysts’ average forecast (the “consensus”). Surprise = (actual − expected) ÷ expected.
  • Guidance: the company’s own forecast. A cut in guidance can outweigh a strong quarter, because investors value future profits.
  • GAAP vs adjusted: “adjusted” or non-GAAP figures leave some costs out. The report has to show how they get from one to the other.

Worked example

Analysts expected EPS of $1.20; the company reported $1.32. The surprise is (1.32 − 1.20) ÷ 1.20 = +10%. If it also lowered its forecast for next quarter, the shares could still fall.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a quarter with the one before when the business is seasonal (holiday sales, for example).
  • Treating adjusted EPS as the whole picture without checking what was left out.
  • Assuming a beat means the stock will rise: guidance and expectations decide the reaction.

Key takeaways

  • Compare with the same quarter last year.
  • What matters is results versus expectations.
  • Guidance often moves the stock most.
