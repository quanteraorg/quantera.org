Introduction to investing

Beginner · Finance basics

The idea

Investing means putting money into something you expect to grow in value, or to pay you income, over time: part of a company (a stock), a loan to a government or company (a bond), or a fund that holds many of these at once. In exchange for the chance of more growth than a savings account, you accept that the value will go up and down, and can fall.

How it works

  • Saving vs investing. A savings account is low-risk and easy to use (in the US, deposits at an FDIC-insured bank are protected up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank), but it grows slowly, and rising prices (inflation) mean the same dollars buy less each year. Investing aims for more growth over many years, with ups and downs along the way.
  • The main building blocks. Stocks: small pieces of companies; the highest long-run growth and the biggest swings. Bonds: loans that pay interest; usually steadier. Cash: low risk, low return. Funds (index funds and ETFs) bundle hundreds of stocks or bonds into one investment, often at low cost.
  • Risk and return go together. Investments that can grow more can also fall further. Spreading money across many companies (diversification) means one bad company can’t sink you.
  • Time is your biggest advantage. Returns earn their own returns (compounding), and a long time horizon gives you room to wait out falls in the market.
  • How markets work. Shares are bought and sold between investors on exchanges such as the NYSE and Nasdaq; the price is wherever buyers and sellers agree. An index like the S&P 500 tracks a group of stocks to show how “the market” is doing.
  • How people start. Open an account with a brokerage (under 18s need a parent or guardian to open a custodial account), invest a set amount regularly, keep fees low, and only invest money you won’t need for several years.
  • Next on Quantera: What is a stock?, then Compound interest and Diversification. Practice with $100,000 of virtual money: no real money is involved.

Worked example

Put away $100 a month for 30 years and you contribute $36,000. If it were invested and earned 7% a year (an assumption for illustration, not a promise), it would grow to about $122,000. Kept as cash earning nothing, it stays $36,000, and if prices rise 3% a year, $36,000 in 30 years buys only about what $14,800 buys today.

Common mistakes

  • Trying to get rich quickly by putting everything into one “hot” stock instead of spreading money out.
  • Selling in a panic after a fall, which turns a temporary drop into a permanent loss.
  • Ignoring fees: 1% a year sounds small but can take a large share of your money over decades.
  • Investing money you’ll need soon: money for the next few years belongs in savings.
  • Treating investing like gambling: frequent trading on tips is closer to betting than to investing.

Key takeaways

  • Investing means owning things you expect to grow, and accepting ups and downs.
  • Higher possible returns come with bigger possible falls.
  • Diversify, for example with a low-cost index fund.
  • Start early, invest regularly, keep fees low, and leave it alone.
  • Practice first: paper trading uses virtual money.
