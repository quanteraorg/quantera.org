Rates and markets

Intermediate · Macro

The idea

Interest rates are the price of borrowing money. When the central bank (the Fed in the US) raises its policy rate, borrowing gets more expensive across the whole economy, and almost every asset price reacts.

How it works

  • Bonds: new bonds pay more, so existing lower-paying bonds fall in price.
  • Stocks: future profits are worth less today when you can earn more in safe assets, and companies pay more on debt. Fast-growing companies, whose profits are far in the future, are hit hardest.
  • Housing: mortgages get pricier, so demand and prices cool.
  • Currency: higher rates attract foreign money, which usually strengthens the dollar.
  • Cuts work the other way round.

Worked example

In 2022 the Fed raised rates from near 0% to over 4%. Bond prices fell sharply, the Nasdaq (heavy in growth stocks) fell about 33%, mortgage rates doubled, and the dollar rose strongly.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking only "the" stock market reacts — sectors react differently (banks can benefit, growth stocks suffer).
  • Forgetting that markets move on expectations: a widely expected hike may barely move prices.

Key takeaways

  • Rates are the price of money and touch every asset.
  • Higher rates: bonds down, growth stocks down, dollar up, housing cooler.
  • Surprises move markets more than expected changes.
