Bond prices and yields

Intermediate · Markets

The idea

A bond is a loan you make to a government or company. It pays a fixed interest payment (the coupon) and returns your money at maturity. Its price moves opposite to market interest rates.

How it works

  • If rates rise to 6%, nobody pays full price for an old bond paying 4% — its price falls until its total yield matches 6%.
  • If rates fall, old higher-coupon bonds become more valuable and their prices rise.
  • Yield is the return you get if you buy at today's price and hold to maturity.

Worked example

A $1,000 bond paying $40 a year (4%). Market rates jump to 5%. To give a buyer about 5%, its price must fall toward roughly $960–$900 depending on years left. Longer bonds fall more.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking bonds cannot lose money — their price can fall a lot when rates rise, especially long bonds.
  • Confusing the coupon (fixed) with the yield (changes with price).

Key takeaways

  • Price and yield move in opposite directions.
  • Longer maturity means bigger price swings.
  • Held to maturity, a high-quality bond still repays its face value.
