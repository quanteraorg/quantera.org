Same news. Different world.

When a story breaks, one company moves 5% and another 25%. Quantera's Insights tool measures how much of each move was the whole market, how unusual it was for that particular stock, and which differences between the companies usually explain the gap.

How a reaction is measured

  1. Day 0 is the first trading session that could react to the news. News published after the 4 p.m. New York close counts from the next session.
  2. The market part is the share of the move the whole market explains: the company's beta (its usual sensitivity to the S&P 500, estimated from up to 120 trading days before the news) times the S&P 500's move that day.
  3. The company-specific part (the abnormal return) is the actual move minus the market part.
  4. A normal day is the usual size of the stock's company-specific moves over the same period. Dividing by it shows how unusual the reaction was for that stock: a 25% move by a stock that often moves 8% a day can be less unusual than a 5% move by one that rarely moves 1%.

What usually explains the gap

  • The whole market (measured): how much each stock tracks the S&P 500.
  • Normal volatility (measured): some stocks always move more.
  • Company size (typical pattern): smaller companies usually react more, because one event can be a larger share of their business, fewer analysts follow them and their shares trade less.
  • Who the news is about (typical pattern): the company named in the story usually moves more than competitors or suppliers.
  • Trading activity (measured): volume compared with the stock's average.
  • Valuation and the run-up before the news (typical patterns): expensive or recently strong stocks can react more to news that changes expectations.

Limits

  • Daily closing prices only, so news during the trading day is measured over the whole day.
  • Other news, earnings or market events on the same day can also move a stock, so these measurements never prove why a price moved.
  • Each comparison is one event per company: a sample of one, which can't show a lasting pattern. No statistical significance test is run; "times its normal day" compares the move with the stock's typical daily size and is not a formal test.
  • Size and valuation are today's figures, not the figures on the day of the news.
  • Education, not investment advice.
